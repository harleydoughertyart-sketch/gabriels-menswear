# The knowledge base

Reference for [`web-dev-from-spine`](SKILL.md). Two jobs: the frameworks that make a spine
**value-driven**, and the question bank that extracts one from a founder who has never articulated it.

Read this before Phase 0. The interview is only as good as the frameworks behind it — a generic question
gets a generic spine, and a generic spine produces a page that could belong to any competitor.

---

## 1. The value equation

Hormozi's, and the reason it belongs here: it turns "make it compelling" into four separately
attackable terms.

```
                 Dream Outcome  ×  Perceived Likelihood
      Value  =  ───────────────────────────────────────
                   Time Delay  ×  Effort & Sacrifice
```

Raise the top, lower the bottom. Every section of a page is doing one of those four jobs — and a
section that does none of them is decoration, which is the sharpest test for cutting one.

**Dream Outcome** — not the feature, not even the benefit: the state of the world afterwards. The
failure mode is stating a capability ("ads for every placement") where the outcome is a condition
("your whole collection is advertised"). Push until the answer is something that changes their life
rather than their workflow.

**Perceived Likelihood** — will it work *for me*? This is where specificity beats adjectives, every
time. Real names, real counts, real before-and-afters, things they can verify in seconds. Superlatives
lower this term; verifiable detail raises it. A visitor discounts everything they cannot check.

**Time Delay** — how long until the first thing they hold. Two ways to lower it and they are not
interchangeable:
- **Structurally** — name the steps that *disappear*. "No studio to book, nothing boxed and posted, no
  waiting on a version." True by construction, unmeasurable, unfalsifiable. Always available.
- **With an anchor** — a familiar unit of time rather than a metric. "Sooner than you would have
  written the caption yourself." Use this when the founder supplies a real figure he stands behind.
  **The best anchor names the task being replaced**, so it measures and reminds in one move. A bare
  number invites arithmetic and doubt; an anchor is felt. It also survives drift — the anchor stays
  true when the real timing moves by 30%, and "in five minutes" becomes a liability.

**Effort & Sacrifice** — what they must learn, hand over, risk or give up. Usually the most
under-attacked term, and often the real reason someone doesn't buy. "You don't have to learn art
direction" is a stronger line than any speed claim.

## 2. Awareness — Schwartz

Where the reader is decides what the page opens with. Getting this wrong is the most common structural
error on a landing page.

| level | they know | the page must open by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Unaware** | nothing | naming the situation so they recognise themselves |
| **Problem-aware** | the pain, not that solutions exist | naming the pain precisely |
| **Solution-aware** | solutions exist, not yours | naming the outcome and how it differs |
| **Product-aware** | yours, not convinced | proof, specifics, objection handling |
| **Most aware** | convinced | the offer and the price |

**Traffic decides this, not taste.** Paid traffic from a cold audience is problem-aware at best; a
comparison search is product-aware. Ask which, and match the hero to it — a hero written for the wrong
level reads as either patronising or baffling.

## 3. The question ladder

**The buyer asks questions in a fixed order. The page answers them in that order.** This is the
structural payoff of the whole framework, and it converts "what sections should we have" from taste
into derivation.

```
(before any question)   What do I even get?
Q1  How do I get that, when I'm not an expert?
Q2  What will mine actually look like?
Q3  How does it work?
Q4  What is it doing for me that I'd otherwise do myself?
Q5  What does it cost me?
Q6  What else is stopping me?
```

Two rules fall straight out of it, and both were learned the hard way:

- **Cost is answered after proof, never before.** A cost argument made before anything desirable has
  been shown answers a question nobody has asked. If a cost section feels flat no matter how it is
  styled, it is in the wrong position — restyling will not save it.
- **One section, one question.** Two sections answering the same question means one gets cut. This is
  the cheapest way to shorten a page without losing an argument.

## 4. Ogilvy, the parts that still bite

- **Specific beats clever.** A concrete noun outsells an adjective. Name the thing.
- **The reader is not a moron.** Condescension and hype both read as contempt.
- **Long copy sells — when the reader wants it.** Length is earned by interest, not assumed. On a
  scanned page, headlines carry the argument and bodies support.
- **Never make a claim you would not make to their face.** This is not ethics, it is conversion: an
  unbelievable claim discounts every believable one beside it.
- **The headline is most of the money.** If the headline needs the body to make sense, the headline
  is wrong.

## 5. Claim discipline

Sort every claim before writing, and keep the buckets:

| bucket | meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **confirmed** | proved in the product, safe to publish, cite it |
| **needs support** | plausible, unproved — omit or qualify |
| **contradicted** | the product says otherwise — a live defect, report it |

Never invent metrics, customer counts, testimonials, timeframes, guarantees or risk reversals. Keep
factual and legal qualifiers — confident writing is not permission to delete them.

**Check for callers, not definitions.** A capability that exists in code but nothing calls is not a
feature you can sell.

## 6. Devices that survived

- **The novice test.** Someone who has never used the product, scanning four seconds, no prior
  knowledge — what do they take away? Write the expected takeaway as one sentence before designing.
  A section needing a caption to be understood has failed.
- **Sell the outcome, not the machinery.** Architecture and modularity are power-user ideas; to a
  first-timer they read as complexity — as work they will have to do.
- **Show, do not assert.** Visual proof beats a claim about visual proof. A wall of finished work
  argues; three struck competitor prices only allege.
- **Name the argument.** "Five different arguments" is abstract until each is named in the buyer's own
  words — the gift, the treat, the decision. Naming makes difference legible.
- **Let the product write its own copy.** Where the app generates copy, run it and pick. Hand-writing
  it bypasses the engine and misrepresents the product on its own site.

## 7. The question bank

Probes, not prompts. Each is designed to get past the answer the founder has already rehearsed. Ask
**one at a time**, and follow the answer before moving on — the second question in a thread is usually
where the real material is.

Pick the two or three per term that fit the business. Do not run all of them; that is an interrogation,
not an interview.

### Dream Outcome

- If this works perfectly, what does their business look like in six months? Not what they'd tell you —
  what actually changes?
- What do they get to **stop doing**?
- What are they quietly hoping for that they'd never say out loud?
- What would they show someone to prove things had changed?

### Perceived Likelihood

- What have they already tried that didn't work? Why would they assume this is the same?
- What's the single most believable thing we could show — something they could verify themselves in
  ten seconds?
- Who do they think this works for, that isn't them?
- What's the most impressive true thing about the product that you've never put on the site?

### Time Delay

- From the moment they say yes, what's the first thing they hold, and how long does it take?
- What steps disappear completely that they're doing today?
- What familiar thing takes about that long? *(this becomes the anchor)*
- Where does it feel slow even when it isn't?

### Effort & Sacrifice

- What do they have to learn? What can they now refuse to learn?
- What do they have to hand over — inventory, access, control, money up front?
- Where in the flow would they give up? What's on screen at that moment?
- What's the part you're embarrassed is still hard?

### Awareness and traffic

- Where are they arriving from, and what did that ad or search promise?
- Do they already know something like this exists?
- What do they type into a search bar at 11pm?

### Difference

- **If a competitor copied this page word for word, which sentence would become a lie?**
  *(If none would, there is no positioning yet and the page cannot be written.)*
- What can you say honestly that nobody else in your category can?
- What do you deliberately not do, that others do?

### Proof

- What can you show that can't be faked?
- What would you show a sceptical friend, in person, to end the argument?
- What's already built that the site has never mentioned?

### Objection

- What makes them close the tab?
- What's the thing they'd need to believe to feel stupid for not trying it?
- What goes wrong often enough that you'd rather address it than hide it?

### The novice

- Describe someone who has never used a tool like this. What do they think it does?
- Which part of how it works confuses people every single time?
- What do you find yourself explaining twice?

### Bounding the work

- What's already good and shouldn't be touched?
- Who supplies the imagery, and what does each image cost you?
- Does this design have to serve other pages? Which?
- What's live right now that you know is wrong?
