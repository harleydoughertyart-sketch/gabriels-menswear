# The ledger

The founder's taste, in his own words, plus the rules those words imply. **This
is the only file in the skill that grows**, and every round inherits it.

Before this existed, taste lived in one conversation and died with it, so every
batch re-learned the same lessons and re-made the same mistakes. A verdict is
cheap to record and expensive to rediscover.

**Adding to it:** only from a real founder reaction to a real rendered ad. Never
from inference, never from a review of your own work, never from a rule you
think follows. Quote him. A paraphrase loses the thing that makes a verdict
reusable.

## The rules

### 1. An ad with no ad in it is not selling the product

> *"I just wish nine's layout had an ad instead. Just the before and after. You
> know what I mean? Because that's what we're trying to sell here. If there's no
> ad in the ad, then it's not really good."*

The product is not a better photograph. It is **a finished, postable ad**. A
before/after pair proves the retouch and stops one step short of the thing being
sold. So the frame must contain a finished ad — visibly a piece of marketing
rather than a product shot.

The exemption is narrow and must be argued: a frame whose entire claim is
fidelity of the piece itself can stay a pair, because there the object *is* the
subject.

It applies to the strongest card in the set too:

> *"If twenty had an ad mixed in there, it would be probably the strongest."*

### 2. No yellow. The brand is pink.

> *"I'm also not really a fan of this yellow. My brand's pink, so we can have the
> background be pink with white grid instead, or we can have it white with the
> pink grid, or we can have it subtly, or like a pop of solid pink maybe, like a
> colour block. I just don't really like the yellow."*

Cream, gold and wheat are out as a GROUND, fine inside a photograph. The three
grounds he named are the whole menu, plus `ink`, which he has never objected to:

| ground | grid | when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `blush` — pink | white lines | warm, editorial, the default |
| `paper` — white | pink lines | clean, lets photography lead |
| `ink` — charcoal | screened | photography glows; keyword flips to `--pink-hot` |

The **colour block** is a flat panel of `--accent-deep` or `--pink-hot` used as
composition rather than decoration. A block the pictures merely sit on is
wallpaper; a block they overlap and break out of is composition.

The site's own background bloom carries an amber lobe, and it was the single
largest source of the cast he rejected. It is gone from the ad shell.

### 3. The wall of evidence is the strongest idea in the set

> *"I like the wall of evidence… I like how we're showing how one piece can turn
> into a bunch of images. Twenty is by far the strongest of all of them."*

One input photograph, many finished outputs, laid out so the multiplication is
visible at a glance. Every print must be recognisably the SAME piece, or it reads
as a stock grid.

### 4. Take the layouts from the marketing site

> *"Do you see how you're taking the design ideas from the marketing page and
> turning them into these? Like maybe some fanned-out versions, like my hero
> section where it has the phone image, the generated image, and then the ad,
> with just some copy at the top."*

The site is the source. The **fanned three-up** — phone photo → generated image →
finished ad, copy above — is a layout he named and wants more of.

### 5. Simple with one big call to action works

> *"Nine's good. I like how it's simple, but there's a really big call to
> action."*

A single large shimmering button is a feature. Small buttons read as footnotes;
one card measured its CTA at 0.4% of the frame and grew it to 13.6% by deleting
four elements that were coasting.

### 6. Boring is a defect, and so is crazy

> *"Seven — I like the idea of having all three there. It just feels a little bit
> too boring."*
> *"Besides that, I'm not really a fan of any of those. Ones too crazy."*

A neat grid of equal panels is boring; five competing devices is crazy. One loud
thing, then support.

### 7. A weak headline sinks a strong layout

> *"Fourteen might be my favourite one. I'm not really a fan of the headline, but
> the rest is really strong. I like the layout."*

Layout and line are judged separately. A layout he likes is reusable with
different copy — and copy is never authored, so a weak line means picking a
different published line, never writing one.

### 8. Speed comes from width, and he says so

> *"This round was fast, and it worked great with all of the agents working on a
> single ad at the same time."*

One agent per card, dispatched together. He rejected the serial version twice
before this: *"Everything's taking way, way, way, way, way too long."*

### 9. The grid line follows the ground's darkness

> *"I'd rather it be a pink grid because it doesn't look like the background's
> dark enough to require the white grid. I'm saying if we get, like, a deep pink
> background, like the button is, we could have a white grid. But if it's normal,
> like a lightish color like this, we could just have the same pink grid. Stays
> more on brand anyway."*

Light ground (`blush`, `paper`) takes the studio's own pink line. White is
reserved for a ground dark enough to need it — `deep`, the button's
`--accent-deep` as a full ground, and `ink`.

**He reported this as the grid painting OVER a headline, and it was not.** At full
resolution the lines stop cleanly at the glyph edges. The artefact lives in the
DOWNSCALE: a white 2px line resampled to 37% for the phone twin lands inside
near-black letterforms and reads as a slice through them. His fix removes the
cause rather than hiding it, because a low-contrast line on a light ground cannot
produce a bright artefact inside dark type.

Worth carrying beyond the grid: **a founder bug report names what he saw, not what
happened.** Reproduce at full resolution before accepting the diagnosis, and check
the phone twin before dismissing the report.

### 10. Never put another company's name on an ad, even as a citation

> *"Remove that if there is a Shopify chip. I don't have... I'm not using
> Shopify."*

Cards 44 and 46 carried a small chip reading "SHOPIFY, 2026" under the struck
$3,000. It was an ATTRIBUTION — Shopify publishes the day rate — and both card
headers argued at length that a struck price with no publisher against it is the
dishonest version. He read it as a statement about which platform he sells on.

**That misreading is the finding.** If the person who commissioned the card reads
a citation as a claim about himself, a stranger scrolling a feed has no chance. A
chip small enough to be mistaken for a logo will be read as one.

The figure itself is untouched and was never in question — $500–$3,000 a day is
founder-set and is the point of that section (*"the 500 3000 is the main point…
the big number should be the saving / value"*), and the supporting line still
states the full range, so the claim stays qualified on its face.

If attribution ever returns it has to be a name a jewellery seller recognises as a
publisher, never one she can mistake for our vendor. This is separate from the
open question about naming a COMPETITOR, which is still unanswered.

### 11. "The right shape" is dead. Say "the right size".

> *"Everywhere you say the right shape, I don't like that. It doesn't sound
> human. Just say the right size instead of shape."*

Said on card 27, and he said **everywhere** — so it binds the whole set and every
future round, not that card. "Shape" is what the layout engine does; "size" is
what a seller asks for. The word that describes our mechanism is almost never the
word the customer uses for her problem, and this is the cheapest possible example
of the difference.

### 12. The key phrase pair goes in pink, and a step larger

> *"Maybe we can just have YOUR PIECE in pink and then READY TO POST in the pink
> and a little bit bigger. I think that'll just add the final touches to it."*
> (card 21)

> *"I just think we need more pink in the wording in the top left. So let's do
> YOUR PIECE, READY TO POST again in the pink font and styling, and just make it
> a tiny bit larger, because it feels a little small. **My website has it
> nice.**"* (card 28)

> *"BRING ONE PIECE should be in the pink font… Just put AD in the pink as well.
> So ONE PIECE and AD in pink."* (card 37)

Three cards, independently, in one pass — which is what makes it a rule rather
than three notes. A headline built as a pair takes the pink on **both** halves,
set a step larger than the line around them.

"My website has it nice" is an instruction, not an aside: the treatment already
exists on `/for/jewelry` and is to be matched, not reinvented.

### 13. Benefits, never features — and a count is usually a feature

> *"SEVEN PLACEMENT SIZES — you should be telling them benefits, not features.
> Like seven placement sounds like a feature. It should be like ADS ANYWHERE YOU
> POST, because that's what it really is. So AD SIZES OPTIMIZED FOR WHERE YOU
> POST — something like that would be better."*

And on card 25, the same instinct against the same shape of line:

> *"SEVEN SIZES, ALREADY SHAPED… ALREADY SHAPED just doesn't feel right. It's
> really trying to sell that it's easy to get ads created for any format that you
> need to run static ad campaigns."*

The tell is a number attached to a capability. "Seven" is a fact about our output;
"anywhere you post" is a fact about her week. He does not object to the number
itself — he said "SEVEN SIZES is fine" — he objects to the number being the
**claim**. Let the count support a benefit; never let it be the benefit.

The same review produced the general form of it on card 29, about copy rather
than placements:

> *"We're selling ease of copywriting. It already writes it for you… it's easy
> for you to get professional ads with almost no work and fast. You're selling
> speed and ease here."*

### 14. Big and few, not small and many — a headline read from across the room

Five of the sixteen finalists drew this note in a single pass, which is what
promotes it from a preference to the rule the next round is designed against.

> *"I really need this to be visible from a distance and not look like a word
> wall. Like, it should be a clear value headline. And then when they start
> reading, they can read the fine print if they want."* (card 51)

> *"I think it could be simpler. Like, we don't need all the words there. It could
> be, like, one phone photo turned into a ready-to-post ad, just to minimize the
> words there… let's get through more versions with the more confined copy that's
> larger. **I feel like that's gonna convert more the bigger we read.**"* (card 28)

> *"I like version b, but I wish the words were just larger."* (card 41)
> *"I like version b the most. I still wish it was a little larger."* (card 36)

**The mechanism is a trade, and he has now made it four times: cut words to buy
size.** Not "set the same copy larger" — that only moves the wrapping. Every
card that drew this note has a copy block doing two jobs at once, and the fix is
to let the headline carry the value alone and demote the rest to fine print that
rewards a reader who has already stopped.

It also explains his A/B picks, which look inconsistent until you apply it: he
took the LARGER variant on every card whose pair differed by size (28, 36, 41),
and the SMALLER-change variant on every card whose pair differed by something
else (21, 25, 27, 29). Size is never the thing he leaves on the table.

The corollary he stated on card 51: *"it should be a clear value headline, and
then when they start reading, they can read the fine print."* There are two
readers, at two distances, and the frame must serve the far one first.

### 15. A white packshot is not a weak ground — it is what brands need

> *"white packshots are key for brands"*

Said while handing over a clean white studio frame of the wave ring to go into
card 23's wall, and it corrects an assumption written into the very first batch:
`v4-00`'s header argued *"a white packshot is the weakest possible ad ground."*
That is true of a frame carrying an ADVERTISEMENT. It is false of a tile in a wall
of evidence, where the white frame is the one a stockist, a marketplace listing or
a lookbook actually asks for.

It also earns its place tonally: the packshot was the only frame on that board
where the ring's rose-gold inlay reads as a second metal instead of a shadow line.
Measured as R−B against each frame's own neutral silver — **+71 on the white
packshot, +53 on the finished ad, no measurable hue at all on the red silk**, where
the channel crushes to rgb 29,1,2. **Even diffuse light is the only condition under
which a small recessed inlay survives**, which is a fact about photographing
jewellery, not about this card.

### 16. One set, once. Three tiles of the same shoot is monotony, not an argument

> *"For version B, we have the main ad, which is from the red velvet image. Then we
> have the red velvet image on its left. And then below, we have a close-up of the
> same red velvet image. So there's **three images, including the ad, that all have
> the same set, which looks terrible.** We wanna use some of the other cleaner sets
> to fill in the space here."*

**This overturned a defence this project had already written down twice.** Card 23
pins a raw workbench snapshot to a wall and anchors it with a finished ad, and two
rounds of notes argued that the pin, the tile it became, and the ad built on that
tile being ONE photograph at three stages was the card's whole argument — the
repeat was called "a strength, on structure not tone", with luminance measurements
offered in support.

He looked at it for four seconds and said it looks terrible. He is right, and the
error is instructive: **the argument was true and invisible.** A viewer does not
know the three frames are one journey; she sees the same red silk three times and
reads it as a set with nothing else to show. A wall of evidence proves RANGE, so
its tiles must differ in the dimension it claims. Repetition inside it argues
against the card.

The rule: on any wall, one set appears once. Two if the second is doing work no
other tile can do, and never three.

### What the picks say, beyond the picks

**He picks the version with the fewest new devices.** On 27 he took the pure word
swap over the version that also fixed the crowding he had complained about. On 21
he took the even step over the clever hierarchy. On 48 he took the one where the
mark is gold and quiet over the ring and the underline. The pattern is not
conservatism — he takes big swings on SIZE every time — it is that an extra
device has to earn its place against doing nothing.

**"We lost the plot a little bit"** (card 36) is the failure mode to watch. That
card took a marker, an underline, a masthead recompose and two copy cuts across two
rounds, each individually justified, and arrived somewhere he did not want. A card
that accumulates correct changes can still drift. When he says a card is close,
stop adding.

**A mark he dislikes, he dislikes immediately and completely.** The brush underline
on 36: *"The underline looks terrible, though. That needs to just be removed."* No
negotiation, no smaller version. Take it out; do not substitute a subtler one.

## The gap in the material — CLOSED 2026-08-26

**It said:** no finished ad of the five-stone pendant exists, so every wall card —
the format he calls strongest — anchored with another piece's ad. Four agents hit
this independently and none closed it. One of them considered composing a pendant
ad in CSS and rejected it correctly: a drawn ad is a diagram of an ad, which is the
whole thing rule 1 exists to prevent.

**It is closed.** The ad was rendered through the app's own ad path — GPT Image 2,
`klein`, `AD_GENERATE_MODE` — at 4:5 and 1:1, six takes, ~$0.60, and lives outside
git at `D:\Luvley Ad Campaign\jewelry-canary\ads\pendant\`. Copy was selected, not
authored: every string is `plans/proof-jwl-premium.plans.json#ad-1`, the plan
whose own source photograph *is* that shot, already published as card 002 — the
one he judged *"I really like two."*

Cards **22, 24, 26 and 46** now anchor on it, so every object in each of those
frames is one piece and rule 3 holds. Three things that pass came out of doing it:

- **A wall card's ad cell decides the file, and only a render tells you which.**
  Card 26's cell is 2x2 of wider-than-tall cells, so the 4:5 file `cover`-crops ~25%
  of its height and slices the Luvley wordmark in half; the square loses 3%. The
  prescription said 4:5 and was wrong.
- **The pendant ad does NOT dissolve on `ink`**, which was the stated reason for
  refusing card 26. It also carries a wordmark, audience line, body copy and a pink
  CTA that the borrowed poster never had, so rule 1 reads *better* on charcoal, not
  worse. A white hairline at 22% on `.tile--ad` is the insurance.
- **The ad's own source print has to move.** All four cards already showed the
  velvet-tray photograph the ad was rendered from, so the swap put the same scene in
  the frame twice.

**One rule-3 break is still open, and it is pre-existing:** card 22's `old-money`
tile is the bar-motif *ring*, not the pendant. It was relocated out of row 1 rather
than replaced.

**Card 50 does NOT want the pendant ad, and the render's own `round.json` was wrong
to name it.** It is not a wall — it is ONE ad shown at seven placement sizes, so
rule 3 never applied to it. What it needs is an ad that survives being 259px wide,
which is a measurable property and not a taste: the halo ad sets its headline at
roughly a quarter of the frame height (~68px cap height in the `Square` tile) where
the sapphire ad measures ~11px and reads as a grey smudge. The pendant ad sets its
headline at a similar proportion to the sapphire's, so swapping it in would break
the one thing that card exists to do. **Leave card 50 alone.**
